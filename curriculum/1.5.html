Applying the EDP

Lesson Objectives

  • Be able to apply their knowledge of the engineering design process to an activity
  • Understand the different steps of the engineering design process and how each of those steps apply to a real-world project
  • Be able to work in groups and gain an understanding of why teamwork is important as an engineer

Lesson Overview

Students in this activity will try one cycle of the Engineering and Design Process, using a VEX activity. Afterwards, they’ll reflect on how they could use the process again in order to improve their designs.

Materials Needed

  • VEX IQ Robotics Kit components
  • Paper and pencil
  • Hang Out Activity

Getting Started

  • Begin with a brief discussion to remind students of the learning from last class, and see if they can name all steps of the Engineering Design Process. At the end of the discussion, tell the students they are going to engage one whole cycle of the EDP with this next activity. (5 mins)
  • Group students to distribute the VEX kits, making sure they don’t open them yet.

  • Discussion Prompts
    • What does EDP stand for? Can you remember all of the steps?
    • What does iterative mean?
    • Is each step completely unmovable, or can you follow the cycle more loosely?

Monitoring Progress

  • To begin, show the class the Hang Out Activity, and pass out the paper and pencils. Ask the class to tell you what the assignment is, or the problem that they’re solving. Then, on their paper, have them write 3 important details about the assignment. (3-5 mins)
  • Once the students finish, have them turn their papers over, and draw at least three different ideas for how to solve the problem. (10 mins)
  • Once they’ve completed their drawings, have each student pick the design they want to construct and give them time to do so. (15 mins)

  • Facilitation
    • The introduction requests are the Ask and Research steps of the EDP - make sure to note that out loud!
    • When testing, make sure the students don’t touch their structures after they set them! If it falls, that’s the result of their test, not a measurement.
    • Encourage their drawings to be as different from each other as possible, and they can have more than 3
    • When building, they aren’t limited to one exact drawing, encourage them to be inspired by their own ideas-but keep prolonged experimentation to a minimum, for the sake of time.

Wrapping Up

  • Once all students are finished constructing, test their designs by measuring from the edge of the table/desk to the furthest point on their structure. (10 mins)
  • After the activity, have students reflect on their designs and their thought processes. Prompt students to consider how they could explore different avenues of approaching innovative solutions if given more opportunities with fewer constraints (time, materials). Encourage students to think about how this activity could be applied to robotics in the building process (as noted in the discussion prompts). (5 mins)
  • Lastly, clean up and ensure that all materials are returned to their respective places and organized properly - if necessary, remove the batteries and controllers for charging (noted below).

  • Discussion Prompts:
    • Ask students where/how they gained inspiration for their ideas
      • Why did they choose the designs they did over the others?
    • Prompt students to discuss how this activity applies to robotics as a whole
      • Why is weight important?
      • What could happen if the weight is unbalanced in a robot or building?
      • Can you think of any other ways this activity might apply to other real-world designs?
    • How were everyone’s designs similar or different?

Assessment

  • Successful completion of activity
  • Brainstorming drawings completed and have clearly different ideas present

Preparation for next class

Next class begins the first major STEM unit for the course, so make sure that all electronics (control hub batteries and controllers) are charged or have a proper area to charge in. Additionally, it is highly recommended to have an area within the classroom where students can save their work that can be an established and undisturbed zone for multiple weeks. The space does not need to be large, merely even just a couple square feet, but it will save a lot of time both for construction and also later in the process when the students are customizing their robots.
