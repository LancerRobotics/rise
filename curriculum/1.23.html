Tug of War

Lesson Objectives

  • Compete in the Up and Over Challenge
  • Identify potential problems they’ll and questions they want answered in the coming weeks
  • Identify the Constraints and Criteria of their challenge in their Engineering Notebooks
    • Understand the difference between what a robot needs to work vs. what they want to add

Lesson Overview

The purpose of this lesson is to introduce a key concept of engineering research to students by way of a fun activity. Using their prior knowledge, they can start to begin identifying the criteria and constraints, helping them solidify their foundation of this topic. Students will use the feedback from the activity as research before they begin their design challenges in the coming weeks.

Materials Needed

  • VEX IQ field materials
  • Students’ Engineering Notebooks
  • ClawBots and Controllers
  • Up and Over Challenge
  • Constraints and Criteria video

Getting Started

  • Begin with a whole group discussion about what the difference is between a want and a need in a design.
    • A "need" refers to essential components or features required for the robot to function effectively, such as motors and sensors for movement and feedback.
    • A "want" includes additional enhancements, like design aesthetics or advanced programming features, that improve performance but are not critical for basic operation.
  • Clarify that needs are what the robot must have in order to function in the challenge, while wants may improve the performance/look of the robot, but are not required.
  • Once finished discussing, show the class the Constraints and Criteria video.
  • After, explain the Up and Over Challenge to students, and answer any questions. Ignore the final instruction about students designing their own robots-for now they’ll use their Clawbots.
    • Be sure to point out:
      • Start and end zones
      • Main goal/task
      • Key timing or scoring rules
    • After questions, dismiss the students to their Bot Groups to retrieve materials and set up the challenge fields.

  • Discussion prompts:
    • What’s the difference between a want and a need?
      • How could this be applied to a design?
    • What’s the difference between a minimum and a constriction?
    • Do design projects have these kinds of limitations?

Monitoring Progress

  • Before they begin the challenge, have students go into their Engineering Notebooks and write down all the Criteria and Constraints they can think of for the final challenge.
    • Ex: “The robot should complete the task quickly” or “The robot must not fall off the ramp”, etc
  • After they finish, have the students complete the activity, and when they finish their match, go back to their Engineering Notebook. Now that they’ve tried out something like their challenge, prompt them to try and come up with more criteria for their list, and potentially even new constraints.

  • Documentation Prompts:
    • What Constraints for your final challenge can you think of?
    • What Criteria for your final challenge can you think of?
    • After competing in the controlled activity, what can you add to your lists? What new criteria/constraints can be added now that you’ve tested it?
      • What about something that you think is important but doesn’t fall under these categories?

Wrapping Up

  • Once students are finished, remind the students that they will be participating in a modified version of this activity for their final challenge, and remind them of the differences between this challenge and their final challenge. Ask them to share what constraints and criteria they have already come up with, and new ones if they think of them. As a final question to consider, ask the students what they think they can design to meet all of those criteria and constraints.

Assessment

  • Successful completion of challenge
  • Identification of criteria and constraints in their Engineering Notebooks
  • Students showed a developed understanding of needs vs. wants in the opening activity

Preparation for next class

Next class begins the true design challenge of the course, so students will require lots of support. Lots of resources will be provided to ensure the instructor is capable of offering any support needed. The students’ testing field probably won’t need to be ready by the first or potentially even second day, but being able to leave it and not have to reconstruct every class will save time, if possible.

Resources

  • Poster Guidelines
    • The Real World Solution poster should consist of, at least:
      • The problem being solved
      • One drawing of the imagined solution
      • Three briefly described details of the solution
      • The method by which the solution solves the problem
      • What type or types of engineering the solution falls under
      • Why the solution falls under that type (or types)
    • This is a minimum, so creativity is highly encouraged and students are free to go over this minimum as much as they like, as long as all the requirements are met
    • Most of all, the poster should be fun to both make and look at.
    • When presenting, all students involved in creating should speak for a fairly even amount of time with the others, but students can split up the subjects in whatever way they please.
